Soravya

Soravya is an event management platform for tech conferences and events around the world. Organisers run their entire event from one dashboard, while attendees get the programme, their personal agenda and networking right on their phone. I designed both sides of the product, the Soravya console and the mobile app attendees use at the event, and I built the front end of both.

Design

Soravya was built to solve the problems organisers face once ticketing is done: managing attendees and everything else that happens during the event. Organisers often struggle to communicate clearly with attendees, whether that’s a change to the programme, news about a side event, or collecting feedback. They also need to learn about the people attending and help them meet each other, so everyone gets the full value of what they paid for. I mapped out all of these problems and turned them into user personas before the design began. That gave me a clear picture of who I was designing for, what they were struggling with, and how design could solve it.

01

What shaped the product

Four findings shaped every design and development decision behind Soravya.

  • Built for every level of tech experience

    A tech conference brings together developers, investors, policymakers and first-time attendees. I didn’t assume everyone is tech-savvy, so every flow is designed to be easy to understand and use, whatever someone’s experience with digital products.

  • Made for busy venues and weak internet

    People use the app while moving between halls, often on crowded Wi-Fi or a weak mobile signal, and on all kinds of phones. So the app loads quickly, keeps working offline and keeps every screen simple to read at a glance.

  • Side events as a core feature

    The competitor platform we reviewed in detail only offered side-event management on its most expensive plan. In Soravya, every organiser gets the full flow, from submission and approval to publishing.

  • Privacy and consent from day one

    Networking only works if people trust it. Attendees choose whether they are visible, their contact details stay private until they accept a meeting, and they can opt out at any time.

02

Who it’s for

Soravya is built for everyone involved in running or attending an event. They share the same goal, a successful event, but each needs something different from the product. I grouped them into four types of users.

  • Attendees

    Join through their invitation email and use Soravya on their phone throughout the event.

    Needs to

    • Know where to be and when
    • Build a personal agenda
    • Get updates when plans change
    • Connect with the right people, if they choose to

  • Organisers

    Run the whole event from the console, usually on a laptop and sometimes on a tablet at the venue.

    Needs to

    • Import and invite attendees
    • Build and update the programme
    • Approve side events
    • Track how the event is going

  • Comms leads

    Keep attendees informed by sending announcements during the event.

    Needs to

    • Send updates quickly
    • See who received them

    They only see what their job needs, which keeps the console simple and attendee data secure.

  • Check-in staff

    Welcome attendees at the entrance and check them in as quickly as possible.

    Needs to

    • Scan a pass or enter its code
    • Find attendees without a pass
    • Print badges on the spot

03

The principles behind every decision

I set these five rules before designing any screens, and used them to settle decisions throughout the project.

  1. 1

    Show only what’s real

    No placeholder numbers or unfinished features presented as finished. When there is no data yet, the screen says so clearly.

  2. 2

    Prioritise the attendee

    Attendee screens are seen by thousands of people at every event, so they received the most design attention.

  3. 3

    Keep screens focused

    People use Soravya while busy, so every element on a screen has to earn its place.

  4. 4

    Always have a fallback

    If the internet drops or a service fails, people see a clear message and a way forward, never a blank screen.

  5. 5

    Respect people’s choices

    No one’s details are shared without their permission, and opting out takes effect within a minute.

05

Key screens in the console

Six of the most important screens, and the design thinking behind each one.

Event Control: everything that needs attention, in one place

The first version of the event home page was a list of eleven links. It showed organisers where they could go, but not what needed their attention. I moved the navigation into the side menu and redesigned the page as Event Control, a single overview of attendee numbers, sign-up progress, check-ins and anything waiting for a decision.

Each section only appears if the person’s role allows them to act on it, so nobody sees cards they can’t use.

Insight: data that is easy to read

Colours in the charts carry meaning. The sign-up funnel moves from grey to amber to blue to green as people get closer to a complete profile. Programme tracks share one purple colour family because no track is more important than another, and red is only ever used for errors.

Insight shows totals but never names individuals. Any group smaller than ten people shows as “<10”, so nobody can be identified from the numbers.

Programme: clear warnings when something is wrong

Most buttons are kept neutral so that only one action stands out on each screen. When something needs attention, such as two sessions booked into the same room, the organiser sees a clear notice explaining the problem.

Experiences: managing side events step by step

Side events move from submitted to approved to published in one table, with capacity tracked along the way. Every status is written in words, like “Awaiting approval” or “Live to attendees”, so organisers never have to guess what a colour means.

Announcements: reaching the right people

Organisers can write an announcement without leaving Event Control. Before sending, they choose who receives it, how urgent it is, and whether it goes to the event workspace, by email, or both.

Every announcement is reviewed before it goes out, so nothing reaches thousands of attendees by accident.

Moderation: keeping networking safe

When an attendee reports someone, the report goes into a moderation queue with a clear response target: 20 minutes for harassment and 2 hours for anything else. Reports that miss their target move to the top, so nothing is forgotten.

If something serious happens, organisers can pause all networking at the event immediately, while the programme and agendas keep working as normal.

06

The attendee app 📱

This is the part of Soravya that every attendee uses, so it received the most attention. The home screen shows today’s agenda and side events. The programme can be filtered by day and track, and any session can be saved in one tap. The People tab suggests who to meet and explains why in plain words, such as “You both listed fintech and settlement”. Once both people agree to meet, the meeting appears with its time and place, and only then are contact details shared. The event pass is always one tap away for check-in.

07

The design system

Design systems that only live in Figma tend to drift away from the real product over time. To avoid that, I built Soravya’s design system directly into the code. Every component uses named colour tokens, and the build fails if anyone uses a colour outside the system. Organisers can add their own brand colour, but Soravya checks that it is readable before saving it.

Development

On the development side, I built the front end of both the console and the attendee app. The console is a Next.js 16 application made mostly of React server components. Pages are rendered on the server, forms send data straight to the server, and each screen keeps its state in the URL. Every page also checks who the user is and what they are allowed to see before showing anything.

105 pages and 71 API routes across the whole platform.

03

Pages that adapt to each user’s role

When Event Control loads, it first identifies the organisation, the user’s session, their role and the event. It turns the role into a list of permissions and then loads every card at the same time. If the user doesn’t have permission for a card, that data is never even requested.

The links to a page and the page itself use the same permission check, so a user is never shown a link to something they can’t open.

04

Privacy built into the code

Reports never show a group of fewer than ten people. Rather than relying on every chart to remember this rule, I created a dedicated data type for people counts. Once a count is hidden, it no longer contains the real number, so it can’t accidentally appear in a log or an export.

A real bug shaped the wording. A hidden percentage once showed as “<10”, which looked like “less than 10%” when the true figure was 100%. Hidden percentages now read “too few to report” instead.

05

Screen state saved in the URL

Details like the selected date range, an open announcement form or a step in the setup guide are stored in the page address. This means the page loads in exactly the right state, the back button works as expected, and organisers can share a link to exactly what they are looking at.

Every value in the address is checked before it is used. If something unexpected comes in, the page simply falls back to the default view instead of breaking.

06

Design rules checked automatically

I turned the design system’s rules into automated tests so they can’t be broken by mistake. One test blocks any component that uses a colour outside the system. Another finds styles that are referenced but never defined. A third fails the build if any screen is missing its loading, empty, error or offline state.

This came from experience: early on, the sign-in button went live invisible because it referred to a colour that didn’t exist.

07

Fast on any phone, even offline

Attendee pages send no client-side JavaScript to the phone, and link prefetching is turned off because it uses up mobile data. A service worker saves the programme and agenda so they can still be read offline, along with a note showing when they were last updated.

After every build, an automatic check measures the size of each page. Any page that hasn’t been categorised gets the strictest limit by default.

08

Testing

Four groups of automated tests run on every update, alongside the regular unit tests.

  • Permissions

    Checks what every role can and cannot do across the platform.

  • Accessibility

    Scans the live build for accessibility issues using axe-core.

  • Screen states

    Confirms every screen has a loading, empty, error and offline state.

  • Mobile usability

    Checks tap target sizes, input zoom, screen edges and that forms keep answers after an error.

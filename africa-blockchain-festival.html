Africa Blockchain Festival

Africa Blockchain Festival brings founders, investors, policymakers, students and builders together in Nairobi. I designed and built the public website for the 2025 edition, then returned for 2026, held at the Sarit Expo Centre from 15 to 17 October. The site is where people learn about the festival and buy their passes, and where sponsors find out how to take part.

Design

The old website was losing ticket buyers at the moment they were ready to pay. People chose a pass on the site and were then sent to a separate ticketing platform that had forgotten their choice. That platform also took cards only and settled in Nigerian naira, while the passes are priced in US dollars and many attendees pay with M-Pesa or stablecoins. My job was to explain the festival clearly to everyone who visits, then take each buyer from choosing a pass to holding a paid ticket without losing them along the way.

01

What shaped the site

Before I designed a single page, I looked at where the old site was losing people and what the festival needed it to do. Four problems stood out, and each one shaped a part of the new site.

  • Ticket buyers were dropping off before paying

    People chose a pass on the old site, then landed on a ticketing platform that didn’t know what they had picked. They had to start again, and many gave up. On the new site, the pass you choose follows you all the way to checkout.

  • Attendees pay in different ways

    The festival draws people from across Africa, but the old gateway only took cards and settled in naira. Anyone paying with M-Pesa or USDC was shut out, and card buyers paid conversion fees. I built one checkout that takes all three.

  • Every visitor comes for something different

    A founder wants to meet investors, an investor wants the speaker list, a student wants an affordable pass and a sponsor wants to know who will be in the room. Each of them gets a clear route from the homepage, with Get Tickets one tap away on every page.

  • No payment can go unrecorded

    Payment providers don’t always confirm a payment straight away, and sometimes the confirmation never arrives. A buyer who has paid and hears nothing is the worst outcome for the festival, so the site checks every unfinished payment every five minutes until it is settled.

03

The principles behind every decision

I set these rules at the start and used them to settle decisions on every page.

  1. 1

    One clear path to a ticket

    Every page leads to the same three steps: choose a pass, register, pay. Sponsors have a separate route, so the two calls to action never compete.

  2. 2

    Never ask for the same choice twice

    The pass chosen on the tickets page arrives already selected in the registration form, and the order summary follows the buyer to checkout.

  3. 3

    Prices live only on the server

    The page can say which pass someone wants, but never what it costs. Every amount sent to a payment gateway is read from one table on the server.

  4. 4

    Always tell buyers what happened to their money

    After paying, buyers see a receipt and get a confirmation email. If a card is declined, the site tells them no money was taken.

  5. 5

    The team updates the content, not the code

    Speakers, sponsors, passes and translations are edited in WordPress using shared styles, so the team can change them without touching templates.

05

Key pages on the site

The pages people used most, from deciding to attend through to paying, in the order a visitor meets them.

Speakers: who will be on stage

For many visitors the speakers decide whether the trip is worth it, so the speakers page is one click from the homepage. Eighty speakers were published for 2026, each with a photo, a role and an organisation.

The agenda, one day at a time

The agenda is split into tabs for the opening evening and the three festival days, so visitors see one day at a time instead of one long list. Fifty-nine sessions were published for 2026 from the same content structure.

Choosing a pass, compared by level of access

The tickets page opens with the dates, the venue and the festival’s numbers, then compares the passes side by side by what each one includes, with one recommended option.

Media passes are free by application, and group and corporate passes go to the sales team, so those two sit apart from the passes people pay for.

A registration form with four fields

The first design asked fourteen questions about each buyer’s role, company and goals. Paying for a pass only needs the pass, a name, an email and a WhatsApp number, so the form was cut to those four.

A summary beside the form keeps the chosen pass, its price and what it includes in view, with a box for a discount code.

Checkout: card, M-Pesa or USDC

Checkout offers two choices in plain words: pay by card in US dollars, or pay with M-Pesa or a stablecoin. The order summary stays on the right, with a link to change the pass if it is the wrong one.

Travel packages for visitors to Nairobi

Many attendees travel from abroad. Travel packages bundle flights, a hotel, the festival ticket and East African tours into one booking, reserved with a $500 deposit, and group bookings have a page of their own.

Development

The website runs on WordPress with Elementor. Every page is built on a staging copy of the site and moved to the live site only after it has been checked, so changes during ticket sales never break the live site. Behind the checkout is ABF Payments, a WordPress plugin I wrote in PHP. It holds the price of every pass, talks to Stripe and Minisend, records every payment and sends each sale to ABF Admin, the festival team’s dashboard.

01

How a pass is paid for

The six steps between a visitor choosing a pass and receiving their ticket. The solid boxes are code I wrote; the dashed boxes are the payment providers.

02

Code organised like the checkout

The plugin is a set of small files, each named after one job in the checkout, so a change to one payment provider never touches another.

About 7,400 lines of PHP across 22 files.

03

One place for every price

Every pass and its price lives in one table on the server. A link can say which pass someone wants, but never what it costs, so nobody can change a price by editing the page. The previous price is stored too, so the site can show early-bird savings from the same numbers.

04

A card payment priced on the server

When a buyer chooses to pay by card, the plugin opens a Stripe checkout for the amount in that table, or a discounted amount it worked out itself. The amount never comes from the browser. Each checkout carries a reference of the festival’s own, so the payment can be matched to its record whichever way the news of it arrives.

05

Every payment settled exactly once

A payment provider normally reports a payment by calling the site, but those calls can arrive late or not at all. So the plugin also asks Stripe and Minisend every five minutes about any payment still waiting. Both routes end in the same function, which ignores a payment that is already settled, so a buyer gets one ticket email whichever route confirms the payment first.

06

Telling buyers when a card is declined

Many banks send a debit alert for a declined card and reverse it a minute later, so a buyer can think they were charged when they were not. The plugin records every decline. The checkout page tells the buyer that no money was taken, the team is alerted straight away, and if the buyer has still not paid thirty minutes later they get an email to help them finish.

07

Keeping payments safe

The plugin has no automated test suite. These are the safeguards built into it instead.

  • Staging first

    Every page and plugin change is built and checked on a staging copy of the site before it reaches the live site.

  • Signed notifications

    A payment notification is accepted only when its signature checks out, and each one is acted on once.

  • Five-minute checks

    Payments nobody has heard about are checked with the provider every five minutes, and abandoned ones are marked every hour.

  • Secrets kept out of the code

    Payment keys live in the server’s configuration, never in the plugin, so nothing secret is deployed with it.

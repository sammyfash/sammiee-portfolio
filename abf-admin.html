ABF Admin

Africa Blockchain Festival 2026 brought founders, investors, policymakers and builders to the Sarit Expo Centre in Nairobi for three days in October. ABF Admin is the private dashboard the festival team used to follow registrations, ticket sales, campaigns and coupons in one place. I designed it and built it, from the first screen to the database.

Design

Every form on the festival website wrote into its own tab of a shared Google Sheet, and payments were recorded somewhere else again. Anyone who wanted to know how many people had registered, who had paid or which campaign brought them in had to open several spreadsheets and count by hand. The team needed those numbers live, in one place, without giving up the sheets they already worked in.

01

What shaped the product

Four findings shaped the dashboard before I designed a screen.

  • The sheets had to stay

    The team already ran the festival from Google Sheets, and every website form wrote into them. The dashboard reads the sheets every hour instead of replacing them, so nobody had to change how they worked.

  • A registration is not a sale

    Many people registered and never paid. Each person’s row shows whether they have paid, abandoned checkout or not started, so the team knows who to follow up with.

  • Numbers people can trust

    A dashboard that quietly falls behind its sheets is worse than none. The overview checks every sheet against what the dashboard holds and says so in a single line.

  • Which partner brought each buyer

    Partners and media shared their own tracking links. The team needed to see which link led to a paid ticket, not only which channel sent visitors.

02

Who it’s for

ABF Admin is used only by the festival team, and access is by invitation. Everyone shares the same goal, a full and well-run festival, but each group comes to the dashboard for something different.

  • Festival leads

    Run the festival and report on how it is going.

    Needs to

    • See registrations and sales at a glance
    • Compare this week with last week
    • Trust that the figures are up to date

  • Marketing and partnerships

    Run campaigns and share tracking links with partners and media.

    Needs to

    • See which link each buyer came from
    • Create discount codes
    • Find people who started checkout and stopped

  • Attendee support

    Answer attendees and chase unfinished payments.

    Needs to

    • Find a person and their payment status
    • Follow up on declined cards
    • Export a list when needed

  • Admins

    Look after the website and who can use the dashboard.

    Needs to

    • Invite people and set their role
    • Approve access requests
    • Connect new sheets and watch the sync

    Team and Settings are shown to admins only.

03

The principles behind every decision

I set these rules at the start and used them to settle decisions on every screen.

  1. 1

    Keep the tools the team already uses

    The sheets remain the team’s own record. The dashboard reads them and adds live numbers on top, so nothing the team relied on was taken away.

  2. 2

    Calmer than the festival brand

    The public site is loud and dark. A tool people read all day needs to be quiet, so the dashboard is white and grey, with the festival’s magenta kept for the current page and small accents.

  3. 3

    Write every status in words

    Paid, Abandoned, In checkout and Not started are written on every tag, so the meaning never depends on colour alone.

  4. 4

    Say when the numbers are behind

    Every page says when its data last synced, and anything that fails is named with the source, the missing rows and the error.

  5. 5

    One row per person

    People often registered more than once. The table shows each person once, with their latest entry and a small count of repeat registrations.

05

Key screens in the dashboard

These are the screens the team used most during the run-up to the festival, in the order they appear in the menu.

The overview: the whole festival on one page

The overview opens on total leads, event registrations and ticket sales, each with its change this week and a small trend line. Below them, a chart of form submissions over the last 30 days and a breakdown by form show where interest is coming from.

A single line under the figures confirms that every sheet matches the dashboard. Website traffic from Google Search sits at the bottom of the page, so the team can see how many people found the festival through search.

Registrations, with who has paid

Each form has its own list, with one row per person. A tag in the first column shows whether they have paid, tried and failed, abandoned checkout, are still in checkout or have not started.

Payment status is matched by email, then by phone, against the sales records, so the team can filter to the people who registered but never paid and follow them up.

Ticket sales, and the cards that were declined

Every paid pass appears here, from card, M-Pesa and USDC payments, with the pass, the amount and the buyer. Declined card attempts have their own count, with how many still need a person to follow up and how many paid later.

Campaigns: which link brought each buyer

Each tracking link shows how many people started checkout, how many paid, how many stopped and the revenue it brought in. The team can compare the links given to different partners instead of relying only on broad channel names.

Coupons the team can create themselves

Discount codes are created, edited and switched off from the dashboard. Each code shows how often it was used, how many people reached checkout with it, the revenue it produced and how much it gave away.

Website requests in one queue

Instead of asking for website changes in chat, the team files a request with a priority. Each request has a status, notes and a timeline of every change, so anyone can see where it stands.

Invitations and access requests

Admins invite people by email and choose their role. Someone without an account can ask for access from the sign-in page, and every admin gets an email to approve or decline it in one tap.

06

The design system

ABF Admin is deliberately calmer than the public festival brand. It uses white and soft grey surfaces, near-black text and the festival’s magenta for the current page and small accents. Magenta text uses a deeper shade, because the bright one is too light to read as text. Status colours always come with a word.

Development

ABF Admin is a Next.js 16 app written in TypeScript, with Tailwind CSS 4 for styling and Supabase for the database and sign-in. Pages are rendered on the server, so figures arrive ready to read. Google Sheets stays the team’s record: a scheduled job copies every sheet into the database each hour, and the festival website’s payments plugin, which I also built, sends each sale as it settles.

03

A sync that keeps edits, deletions and restores straight

Every hour the sync reads each sheet and fingerprints every row. A new fingerprint is inserted, one that has disappeared from the sheet is marked as deleted rather than erased, and one that comes back is restored instead of duplicated. An edited row simply becomes a new fingerprint, so the old version is retired and the new one added.

04

A check that says when the numbers are wrong

Once, two paid card sales sat in the sales sheet for eleven hours without reaching the dashboard, behind an error nobody could read. After that, every page compares the rows in each sheet with the rows the dashboard holds. If a source is failing, missing rows or out of date, the overview names it, with the gap and the actual error.

05

Coupons counted only when a payment settles

Discount codes are applied by the payments plugin on the festival website, which I wrote in PHP. The browser can name a code but never an amount, so the price is always worked out on the server. A use is counted only when the payment settles, so an abandoned checkout never uses up a limited code.

06

From a tracking link to a paid ticket

A tracking link sets a 30-day cookie when someone first arrives, and the plugin copies it onto their checkout on the server. The campaign report then counts, for each link, how many people started checkout, how many paid, how many did not, and the revenue, which the dashboard shows on the Campaigns page.

07

Keeping the numbers trustworthy

ABF Admin has no automated test suite. These are the safeguards built into the product instead.

  • Sync check

    Every sheet is compared with the dashboard, and a failing, missing or stale source is named on the overview.

  • Failure emails

    The team is emailed when a source starts failing, once, rather than on every hourly run.

  • Secret-checked pushes

    Sales and campaign data from the website are accepted only with a shared secret, and a sale is never recorded twice.

  • Nothing erased

    Rows removed from a sheet are marked as deleted, never erased, so they can come back without duplicates.

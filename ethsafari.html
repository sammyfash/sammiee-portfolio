ETHSafari

ETHSafari is a nine-day Ethereum event that travels from Nairobi to the Kenyan coast at Kilifi, with a train ride, the Blocktrain, in between. For its fifth-anniversary edition I set a new art direction, built a design system in Figma and rebuilt the website natively in WordPress and Elementor.

Design

Most crypto conferences look the same: dark backgrounds, neon gradients and glowing 3D shapes. ETHSafari is different. It moves across Kenya for nine days, from a bootcamp in Nairobi, onto a train, to a festival on the coast, and it has a sense of humour about itself. The website had to feel like that journey, sell sponsorship and applications for an edition whose dates were still changing, and stay easy for a small team to update in the weeks before opening day.

01

What shaped the site

Four problems decided the look of the site and how it is put together.

  • Crypto events all look alike

    Dark pages with neon gradients are the default for web3 events, so a visitor can’t tell one from another. I tried that direction first and set it aside. ETHSafari now has a bright, flat look of its own: cream paper, heavy ink outlines, hard shadows and one strong magenta.

  • The journey is the product

    People aren’t buying a seat in a hall. They are signing up for nine days that move from Nairobi to Kilifi by train. So the route leads the homepage, with each stop, its coordinates and what happens there, before the programme and the ways to take part.

  • Plans change close to the event

    Speakers, sessions and even dates move right up to opening day. When the 2026 edition was postponed, every date on the site changed to TBA and every ticket button became a link to support the event, in eighteen small edits, without rebuilding a page.

  • More ways to take part than a ticket

    Vendors, volunteers and startups all want a place on the journey. Each now has its own page with a short application form, so the team receives applications in one inbox instead of scattered messages.

03

The principles behind every decision

I set these at the start, and they settled most decisions on the site.

  1. 1

    Bright and flat, never neon

    Cream paper, ink outlines and hard shadows with no blur. No gradients, no glass and no 3D, so ETHSafari never looks like another crypto conference.

  2. 2

    The route leads

    Nairobi, the Blocktrain and Kilifi structure the homepage, the schedule and the social posts, so the journey is the first thing people understand.

  3. 3

    Say it with a wink

    The site keeps the event’s own humour, in lines like “Gas: low · Vibes: high” and “Karibu, everyone rides the Blocktrain”, so it sounds like ETHSafari and not a template.

  4. 4

    One action per page

    Each page asks for one thing: support the event, apply to speak, or start an application. The button always says exactly what happens next.

  5. 5

    The team edits content, not code

    Every page is built from Elementor’s own widgets and the site’s global styles, so the team can change any word, photo or link without a developer.

05

Key pages on the site

The pages that tell ETHSafari’s story and ask people to take part, in the order most visitors meet them.

Speakers: five years of voices

The speakers page celebrates everyone who has taken the stage over five editions, from founders to community leaders, and closes with the 2026 call for speakers and a short form to apply.

A schedule that can change

The schedule explains the shape of the week, talks, workshops, hackathons, the Blocktrain and the coastal festival, while the full programme is published in phases. For live updates during the event it sends attendees to Sola Day, where they can RSVP and follow sessions, and keeps past schedules one click away.

A page for vendors

Vendors get a page that speaks their language: nine days, five stops and thousands of builders, founders and travellers to sell to. The application form asks only what the team needs to say yes.

Startup booths in front of the herd

The startup booth page sells the exhibition floor as the place where investors, customers and talent meet, and walks a founder through the application step by step.

Volunteers who keep it running

Volunteers are the reason the Blocktrain leaves on time. Their page lists the teams they can join, from logistics and the stage to hospitality and media, before the application form.

Getting in touch with the team

Questions, partnerships, side events and press all start on one contact page, with a promise that the team will reply as soon as possible.

06

The design system

ETHSafari’s look is flat neo-brutalist: cream paper, near-black ink outlines, hard offset shadows with no blur, and magenta as the one loud colour, with plum for dark sections. In Figma I built it as 18 base colours, 26 named colour roles, 19 text styles and three hard-shadow styles, then set up the same colours and type as global styles in Elementor.

  • Paper #fbf7f2 page background
  • Ink #0d0a12 outlines and hard shadows
  • Magenta #f330e2 the brand colour and buttons
  • Plum #2b0a28 dark sections and headings
  • Amber #ffb247 highlights
  • Pink tint #fce3f7 pale surfaces

Typeface

Aa

Poppins + Archivo + IBM Plex Mono

Poppins Black sets the headlines, Archivo sets body text, and IBM Plex Mono sets the small technical labels and coordinates.

Standards every page meets

  • Every shadow is hard, with no blur
  • Magenta is kept for actions and one word per headline
  • Every page is built from Elementor’s own widgets, with no HTML widgets
  • Every page is designed for a phone as well as a laptop

Development

The site runs on WordPress with Elementor Pro. Every page is built on a staging copy first, then pushed to the live site through a review-and-approve step, so nothing reaches visitors unchecked. Each page is made from Elementor’s own widgets and bound to the site’s global colours and type, with a small amount of scoped CSS only for the effects Elementor has no setting for.

How a change reaches the live site

The path every page and every edit takes. The solid boxes are the steps I own; the dashed box is the organisers’ approval.

02

Built from Elementor’s own parts

Every page shares the same header, footer and global styles, so a change in one place reaches the whole site.

Real WordPress menus throughout, so the team edits navigation in one place.

03

Native widgets, not pasted HTML

A design this distinctive is easy to rebuild as one big block of HTML, but then nobody can edit it in Elementor. Every page is made from real headings, text, buttons, images and menus instead. When it was first built, the homepage alone was 93 containers and 109 headings, with no HTML widgets at all.

04

Brand values the whole site reads

The page maps Elementor’s global colours and fonts to short names once, and every section uses those names. Changing the magenta in the site settings changes it everywhere.

05

Postponing without a rebuild

When the 2026 edition was postponed, every date had to become TBA and every ticket button had to point to Artizen instead. I made eighteen small edits to the existing widgets, changing only their words and links, so every page kept its styles and stayed fully editable.

06

Getting blocked scripts running again

The chat assistant worked on staging but not on the live site. The cause was a security rule in the live server’s configuration that blocked every outside script, which had also silently stopped the site’s analytics. Listing the trusted sources brought back the chat, Google Tag Manager and site statistics, with no errors in the console.
